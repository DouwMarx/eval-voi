## 1. Combining several elicited distributions: linear opinion pool, quantile averaging, single fit

Sources: [stone1961opinionpool], [genest1986combining], [clemen1999combining], [mcconway1981marginalization], [winkler1968consensus], [cooke1991experts], [colson2018classical], [cooke2008tudelft], [lichtendahl2013quantiles], [hora2004calibration], [ratcliff1979vincent], [thomas1980family], [oakley2026shelf]. Clemen and Winkler 1985 and Clemen 1989 are already in research/bib/ensemble.bib.

Quoted statements.

- Stone 1961, abstract (Project Euclid and OpenAlex, 2026-10-01): "When a group of k individuals is required to make a joint decision, it occasionally happens that there is agreement on a utility function for the problem but that opinions differ on the probabilities of the relevant states of nature." The rule: "Choose weights lambda_1, ..., lambda_k (lambda_i >= 0, sum lambda_i = 1); construct the pooled density function p_{s lambda}(theta) = sum lambda_i p_{s i}(theta); choose the d, say d_{s lambda}, maximising u[d | p_{s lambda}(theta)]. This rule, which may be called the Opinion Pool, can be made democratic by setting lambda_1 = ..." (equal weights).
- Clemen and Winkler 1999, full text pp. 189-190 (author copy, Duke, 2026-10-01): "An appealing approach to the aggregation of probability distributions is the linear opinion pool, so named by Stone (1961), and dating back to Laplace (Bacharach, 1979): p(theta) = sum_{i=1}^n w_i p_i(theta), where n is the number of experts, p_i(theta) represents expert i's probability distribution for unknown theta, p(theta) represents the combined probability distribution, and the weights w_i are non-negative and sum to one." "The linear opinion pool clearly is just a weighted linear combination of the experts' probabilities, and as such it is easily understood and calculated. ... Of particular note, the linear opinion pool is the only combination scheme that satisfies the marginalization property (MP)." "Another typical combination approach uses multiplicative averaging and is sometimes called a logarithmic opinion pool. ... If the weights are equal (to 1/n), then the combined distribution is proportional to the geometric mean of the individual distributions."
- Clemen and Winkler 1999, full text pp. 198-200: "simple combination rules (e.g., a simple average) tend to perform quite well. More complex rules sometimes outperform the simple rules, but they can be somewhat sensitive, leading to poor performance in some instances." "Regarding mathematical combining procedures, we believe that simple rules will always play an important role, because of their ease of use, robust performance, and defensibility in public policy settings where judgments about the quality of different experts are eschewed. Simple rules do not, however, allow explicit consideration of factors such as overconfidence and dependence among experts." "When little information is available about the relative quality of and dependence among the experts' probabilities, a simple rule such as a simple average is recommended." "Experts who are very similar (in modeling style, philosophy, access to data, etc.) tend to provide redundant information ... Thus, heterogeneity among experts is highly desirable. ... Makridakis and Winkler (1983) and Clemen and Winkler (1985) demonstrate the diminishing marginal returns associated with large numbers of experts. Their analyses, further supported by Ferrell (1985), suggest using three to five experts."
- McConway 1981, abstract (OpenAlex, 2026-10-01): "The assumption that finding the consensus distribution commutes with marginalization of the distributions implies that the consensus must be found using a linear opinion pool (weighted average), provided the space being considered contains three or more points."
- Genest and Zidek 1986, abstract (Project Euclid, 2026-10-01): "This paper addresses the problem of aggregating a number of expert opinions which have been expressed in some numerical form in order to reflect individual uncertainty vis-a-vis a quantity of interest. ... A taxonomy of solutions is presented which serves as the framework for a survey of recent theoretical developments in the area." (Full text not read: the Euclid PDF is a scan without a text layer.)
- Winkler 1968, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "the decision maker must somehow combine the distributions assessed by the experts and form a single distribution to be used as an input to a formal Bayesian analysis."
- Cooke 1991, publisher abstract (Crossref/OUP, 2026-10-01): the book considers "how the views of several experts might be combined" and "argues for the importance of developing practical models with a transparent mathematical foundation for the use of expert opinion in science". Colson and Cooke 2018, full text pp. 4 and 8 (Strathclyde open copy, 2026-10-01): "experts typically state their fifth, fiftieth, and ninety-fifth percentiles for the estimate of each uncertain item (Cooke 1991; Cooke and Goossens 2008)"; "The classical model averages probability densities."; "Although most approaches (including the classical model) average distributions, Lichtendahl, Grushka-Cockayne, and Winkler (2013) argue that averaging quantiles may be the preferred approach because it concentrates more of the resulting distribution around the median, thus reducing the alleged problem of wide uncertainty distributions in combined assessments. Although this approach may sound appealing, we show later that averaging quantiles rather than distributions has substantial performance costs." Footnote 13: "Averaging quantiles is the same as harmonically weighting the experts' densities (Bamber, Aspinall, and Cooke 2016; Colson and Cooke 2017)."
- Lichtendahl, Grushka-Cockayne and Winkler 2013, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "In terms of location, the two average forecasts have the same mean. The average quantile forecast is always sharper: it has lower variance than the average probability forecast. Even when the average probability forecast is overconfident, the shape of the average quantile forecast still offers the possibility of a better forecast. Using probability forecasts for gross domestic product growth and inflation from the Survey of Professional Forecasters, we present evidence that both when the average probability forecast is overconfident and when it is underconfident, it is outperformed by the average quantile forecast."
- Hora 2004, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "It is shown by theoretical argument that combining well-calibrated distributions of individual experts using linear rules can only result in reducing calibration. In contrast, it is demonstrated, both by example and empirically, that an equally weighted linear combination of experts who tend to be 'overconfident' can produce distributions that are better calibrated than the experts' individual distributions. Using data from training exercises, it is shown that the improvement in calibration is rapid as the number of experts is increased from one to five or six".
- Ratcliff 1979, abstract (OpenAlex, 2026-10-01), the origin of the word Vincentizing: "The quantiles are then averaged over subjects to give group quantiles (cf. Vincent learning curves). ... It is shown that this method of averaging is exact for certain distributions (i.e., the resulting distribution belongs to the same family as the individual distributions)." Thomas and Ross 1980 (title only, Crossref, 2026-10-01): "On appropriate procedures for combining probability distributions within the same family".
- SHELF 1.13.0 reference manual (CRAN, 2026-10-01): fitdist "fits parametric distributions using least squares on the cumulative distribution function. If separate judgements from multiple experts are specified, the function will fit one set of distributions per expert"; linearPoolDensity "evaluates a (weighted) linear pool" of those fits. The standard elicitation software does exactly what voi_rank does: one fit per elicitation, then a linear pool.

What each method does to disagreement (derivation; Lichtendahl et al. 2013 and Ratcliff 1979 for the published versions). Let member i have fitted distribution F_i with mean m_i and variance v_i, N members, equal weights.

- Linear opinion pool (mixture): mean = mean_i(m_i); variance = mean_i(v_i) + Var_i(m_i) by the law of total variance. The second term is the disagreement between the members' centres. Two members who disagree give two modes. Each member's own width survives.
- Quantile averaging (Vincentization): Q(u) = mean_i Q_i(u). For a location-scale family (normal, or lognormal on the log scale) the result stays in the family with location mean_i(mu_i) and scale mean_i(sigma_i) (Ratcliff 1979), so its variance is (mean_i sigma_i)^2 <= mean_i(sigma_i^2), and the Var_i(m_i) term is absent. Always unimodal. Disagreement about the centre is averaged away and leaves no trace in the width. This is Lichtendahl's "always sharper".
- One distribution fitted to all 3N quantile points by least squares. For the lognormal, least squares on log quantiles is linear in the data, so the single fit returns exactly mu = mean_i(mu_i) and sigma = mean_i(sigma_i): it is Vincentization of the log quantiles (geometric averaging of the quantiles) under another name, and the disagreement shows up only as a large fit residual. For the Beta the fit is nonlinear and the result is close to the same. A single Beta or lognormal is forced to be unimodal with one width; when members disagree it sits where nobody believes.

Which the literature recommends for decision analysis. The linear pool is the default in decision analysis and risk analysis: it is the only rule consistent with marginalization (McConway 1981; Clemen and Winkler 1999), it is what the classical model and SHELF compute, and Clemen and Winkler recommend the simple average "when little information is available about the relative quality of and dependence among the experts' probabilities", which is our situation with LLM members. Its known cost is underconfidence when the members are themselves calibrated (Hora 2004); its known benefit is that it corrects overconfident members, which LLM elicitors are (research/elicitation_lit.md: Riegler, Nafar, OpenEstimate). Quantile averaging is recommended in the forecasting literature for sharpness and calibration (Lichtendahl et al. 2013) and rejected in structured expert judgement for "substantial performance costs" (Colson and Cooke 2018). We want the disagreement term, because the spread of the ranking under disagreement is the uncertainty statement the paper makes.

Why it matters for us. docs/DESIGN.md section 6 defines the pooled belief as the equal-weight mixture over fitted elicitations, i.e. the linear opinion pool. docs/QUESTIONS.md section 5 states the alternative (quantile averaging) hides disagreement; the derivation above and Lichtendahl's abstract make that precise. The "three to five experts" remark of Clemen and Winkler 1999 and Hora's "five or six" support the ensemble size chosen in research/ensemble.md.

Paragraph for docs/QUESTIONS.md section 5. We combine the N valid elicitations of a parameter as an equal-weight mixture of their fitted distributions: to draw a value, pick an elicitation at random, then draw from its fitted Beta or lognormal. This is the linear opinion pool, named by Stone (1961), the only pooling rule that commutes with marginalisation (McConway 1981), the rule the classical model of expert judgement and the SHELF software compute, and the one Clemen and Winkler (1999) recommend when nothing is known about the relative quality of the elicitors. Its variance is the average of the elicitations' own variances plus the variance of their medians; the second term is the disagreement, and it is what makes two elicitors who put the prior at 0.1 and 0.6 produce a bimodal pooled belief rather than a hump at 0.35. The alternative of averaging the quantiles (Vincentization) keeps the shape of the family and is always sharper (Lichtendahl, Grushka-Cockayne and Winkler 2013), because it drops the disagreement term; fitting one lognormal to all 3N quantile points is the same thing on the log scale. Forecasting competitions reward that sharpness; a ranking under uncertainty must not, because the question we ask of the Monte Carlo is exactly how far the elicitors' disagreement moves the ranking. The cost of the linear pool is that it is wider than any calibrated elicitor would be (Hora 2004); for overconfident elicitors, which LLMs are, that widening is a correction.

## 2. Monte Carlo propagation of parameter uncertainty in EVSI and PSA

Sources: [ades2004evsi], [strong2015evsi], [heath2020evsi], [kunst2020evsi], [brennan2007partial], [briggs2006decision], [claxton1999irrelevance], [felli1998evpi], [hoffman1994propagation], [jackson2019voi], [howard1966information].

Quoted statements.

- Ades, Lu and Claxton 2004, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "This article derives simple Monte Carlo, or nested Monte Carlo, methods that extend the use of EVSI calculations to medical decision applications with multiple sources of uncertainty ... The authors describe Monte Carlo procedures for calculating EVSI for probability, rate, or continuous variable parameters in multi parameter decision models".
- Strong, Oakley, Brennan and Breeze 2015, full text (PMC4471064, 2026-10-01), equation 4: "EVSI = E_X[max_d E_{theta|X}{NB(d, theta)}] - max_d E_theta{NB(d, theta)}"; "EVSI is commonly estimated via a 2-level Monte Carlo procedure in which plausible data sets are generated in an outer loop, and then, conditional on these, the parameters of the decision model are updated via Bayes rule and sampled in an inner loop. At each iteration of the inner loop, the decision model is evaluated."; "A probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) takes N samples from the joint distribution of the input parameters."
- Brennan, Kharroubi, O'Hagan and Chilcott 2007, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "The mathematical definition shows 2 nested expectations, which must be evaluated separately because of the need to compute a maximum between them. ... Maxima of Monte Carlo estimates of expectations are biased upward, and the authors show that the use of small samples results in biased EVPI estimates."
- Heath et al. 2020, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "Estimating EVSI with the standard nested Monte Carlo algorithm has a notoriously high computational burden, especially when using a complex decision model or when optimizing over study sample sizes and designs." Kunst et al. 2020, abstract (PubMed, 2026-10-01): "Historically a computationally expensive method to compute the expected value of sample information (EVSI) restricted the use of VOI to simple decision models and study designs."
- Claxton 1999, abstract (PubMed, 2026-10-01): "Decisions should be based only on the mean net benefits irrespective of whether differences are statistically significant ... The distribution of net benefit is only relevant to deciding whether more information is required."
- Felli and Hazen 1998, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "EVPI ... incorporates both the probability of a decision change and the marginal benefit of such a change into a single measure, and therefore provides a superior picture of problem sensitivity."
- Hoffman and Hammonds 1994, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "Two distinctly different approaches using Monte Carlo methods are discussed: (1) the end point is a fixed but unknown value ... or (2) the end point is an unknown distribution of values ... In the first case, values are sampled at random from distributions representing various 'degrees of belief' about the unknown 'fixed' values of the parameters to produce a distribution of model results. The distribution of model results represents a subjective confidence statement about the true but unknown assessment end point. The important input parameters are those that contribute most to the spread in the distribution of the model results."
- Howard 1966, abstract (OpenAlex, 2026-10-01): "no theory that involves just the probabilities of outcomes without considering their consequences could possibly be adequate in describing the importance of uncertainty to a decision maker. ... It is found that numerical values can be assigned to the elimination or reduction of any uncertainty. Furthermore, it is seen that the joint elimination of the uncertainty about a number of even independent factors in a problem can have a value that differs from the sum of the values of eliminating the uncertainty in each factor separately."
- Briggs, Claxton and Sculpher 2006 (Open Library record, 2026-10-01): "This volume deals with the key techniques and approaches that can be used to estimate the cost-effectiveness of health care interventions." The standard textbook treatment of PSA and VOI by Monte Carlo; text not read.

The distinction the paper must make (derivation, with the sources above for the pieces). Write phi = (p, s, t, B, K) for the decision-level and instrument-level parameters other than cost. Three numbers carry the name EVSI and they differ.

1. EVSI at known parameters, EVSI(phi): the closed form of docs/DESIGN.md section 3. It is the value of one run to a developer whose belief is phi. Our Monte Carlo evaluates it at every draw phi_d from the pooled belief. The output is a distribution of EVSI over the pooled belief about phi. In Hoffman and Hammonds' terms this is the first kind of Monte Carlo: the end point (the worth of the evaluation) is a fixed but unknown number, and the spread is "a subjective confidence statement" about it driven by which elicitation is right. Its mean, E_phi[EVSI(phi)], is the expected worth to a developer who would learn phi before deciding. The health-economics inner loop (expectation over theta given the data) is done analytically by the closed form; only the outer loop over phi is sampled. The structure "expectation outside, maximum inside" (Strong et al. 2015, eq. 4; Brennan et al. 2007) is what makes 1 and 2 differ.
2. The developer's EVSI under the pooled belief: a developer who adopts the pooled mixture as its own belief does not know phi and decides on the predictive probabilities. With the eight parameters drawn independently (docs/DESIGN.md section 6), P(theta = 1, x = 1) = E[p s] = pbar sbar, P(theta = 0, x = 1) = (1 - pbar)(1 - tbar), and the payoff of responding given x is Bbar P(theta = 1 | x) - Kbar P(theta = 0 | x) because B and K are independent of (theta, x). Hence EVSI_pool = EVSI(pbar, sbar, tbar, Bbar, Kbar) exactly: the closed form at the pooled means. It is one number, as EVSI always is for one decision-maker (Claxton 1999: the distribution of net benefit matters only for deciding whether to seek information). It can be zero (the pooled developer deploys regardless or responds regardless) while E_phi[EVSI(phi)] is positive because some elicitations can change the decision. P(EVSI > 0) over draws is therefore "the probability that the result can change the decision of a developer holding one of the elicited beliefs", not a statement about the pooled developer.
3. The central estimate: the closed form at the pooled medians (docs/DESIGN.md section 2). For the lognormal B and K the mean exceeds the median by exp(sigma^2/2) (a factor 2.7 when q95/q05 = 100), and for a skewed Beta the two also differ, so the central estimate is a third number: a robust central estimate, not EVSI_pool. The paper should name which one each table reports. Suggested wording: point tables use the central estimate; uncertainty uses the draws (number 1); EVSI_pool (number 2) can be added as one column, at no cost, as the belief-consistent value.

No general ordering holds between 1 and 2: EVSI(phi) is a difference of piecewise-linear convex functions of p, not itself convex, so Jensen's inequality does not apply (derivation).

Why it matters for us. A reviewer who knows the health-economics literature will ask whether our "distribution of EVSI" is the EVSI. The answer is that the draws are a PSA (Strong et al. 2015) of the pricing, in the sense of Hoffman and Hammonds' first approach and Felli and Hazen's use of VOI as a sensitivity measure, and that the single developer's EVSI under the pooled belief is the closed form at the pooled means. Howard's last sentence (joint elimination of several uncertainties is not additive) is the one-line citation for the "batteries of evaluations" limitation in docs/DESIGN.md section 3.

Paragraph for docs/QUESTIONS.md (new section on Monte Carlo and summaries). The health-economics EVSI literature computes the value of a study by a two-level Monte Carlo: an outer loop over possible data, an inner loop over the parameters given those data, with a maximisation over decisions between the two (Ades, Lu and Claxton 2004; Strong et al. 2015). Our decision has one binary state and one binary result, so the inner level is a closed form and only the outer level over the elicited parameters is sampled. What the 100,000 draws give is not one EVSI but the spread of EVSI across the beliefs the ensemble holds: a probabilistic sensitivity analysis of the price, in the sense in which Felli and Hazen (1998) use value of information as a sensitivity measure, and in the sense of Hoffman and Hammonds' (1994) Monte Carlo for a fixed but unknown end point. The developer who adopted the pooled belief would have one EVSI, which under independent parameters is the closed form at the pooled means; the central estimate at the pooled medians is a robust stand-in for it, and the two differ when B and K are skewed. We report the central estimate for points, the draws for uncertainty, and say so.

## 3. Rank uncertainty from Monte Carlo draws: rank probabilities, SUCRA, rankograms, P(top-k)

Sources: [salanti2011graphical], [rucker2015pscore], [trinquart2016uncertainty], [marshall1998league], [goldstein1996league].

Quoted statements.

- Salanti, Ades and Ioannidis 2011, abstract (PubMed PMID 20688472, 2026-10-01): "We present the results under various forms, conventionally based on the mean of the distribution of the effect sizes; based on predictions; based on ranking probabilities; and finally, based on probabilities to be within an acceptable range from a reference. We show how to obtain and present results on ranking of all treatments and how to appraise the overall ranks. Bayesian methodology offers a multitude of ways to present results from MTM models, as it enables a natural and easy estimation of all measures based on probabilities, ranks, or predictions." (Full text not read; the definitions below are Rücker and Schwarzer's restatement of Salanti's.)
- Rücker and Schwarzer 2015, full text (PMC4521472, 2026-10-01), Background: "For each treatment i (i = 1, ..., n) the probability P(i, k) that treatment i has rank k (k = 1, ..., n) is computed"; with cumulative rank probabilities F(i, r) = sum_{k <= r} P(i, k), "SUCRA(i) = 1/(n - 1) sum_{r=1}^{n-1} F(i, r)". "It is not sufficient and can be misleading to solely look at the probability of being best, as it does not take uncertainty into account." Methods: "we may interpret P-bar_i as the mean extent of certainty that mu_i is greater than any other mu_j, averaged over all competing treatments j" (P-bar_i is the P-score of treatment i). Conclusions: "Like the SUCRA values, P-scores induce a ranking of all treatments that mostly follows that of the point estimates, but takes precision into account. However, neither SUCRA nor P-score offer a major advantage compared to looking at credible or confidence intervals."
- Trinquart et al. 2016, abstract (PubMed PMID 27089537, 2026-10-01): "The median width of the 95% CrIs of the SUCRA was 65% (first to third quartile, 38% to 80%). In 28% of networks, there was a 50% or greater probability that the best-ranked treatment was actually not the best."
- Marshall, Sanderson, Spiegelhalter and McKee 1998, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "There was great uncertainty, however, concerning the true ranks, particularly for the smaller clinics. Only one clinic could be confidently ranked in the bottom quarter according to this measure of performance. ... Even when there are substantial differences between institutions, ranks are extremely unreliable statistical summaries of performance". Goldstein and Spiegelhalter 1996 (title verified, Crossref, 2026-10-01) is the statistical source for rank intervals from posterior draws.

Mapping to our summaries (derivation). On each draw d every evaluation gets a rank by eta. The matrix P(i, k) = fraction of draws on which evaluation i has rank k is the rankogram. F(i, k) = P(rank_i <= k) is the cumulative ranking probability and is exactly our P(rank <= k), i.e. P(top-k). The 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of rank_i over draws are the rank intervals of Marshall et al. SUCRA(i) = (n - E[rank_i])/(n - 1), because sum_{r=1}^{n-1} F(i, r) = n - E[rank_i]. Without ties, E[rank_i] = 1 + sum_{j != i} P(eta_j > eta_i), so SUCRA(i) = mean_{j != i} P(eta_i > eta_j): the Monte Carlo SUCRA is the average of our pairwise outranking probabilities, which is Rücker and Schwarzer's P-score computed from draws instead of normal theory. Ties at eta = 0 (the result cannot change the decision on that draw) are given the average rank, as docs/DESIGN.md section 6 says; with ties, use P(eta_i > eta_j) + 0.5 P(eta_i = eta_j).

Why it matters for us. docs/QUESTIONS.md section 7 names "rank probabilities" and "cumulative ranking curves" from NMA; this gives the exact definitions and the identity that makes the pairwise table, the rank intervals and SUCRA one object. Trinquart's and Marshall's findings are the precedent for reporting that a ranking is undecidable at the available precision. Rücker and Schwarzer's conclusion supports putting rank intervals in the appendix and the group comparison A in the headline.

Paragraph for docs/QUESTIONS.md section 7. On every Monte Carlo draw each evaluation has a rank by eta, so the draws give, per evaluation, the probability of each rank (the rankogram of network meta-analysis, Salanti, Ades and Ioannidis 2011), its cumulative form P(rank <= k), and rank percentiles (the rank intervals of Marshall et al. 1998, who showed that institutional league tables have wide rank intervals even when the institutions differ). The surface under the cumulative ranking curve, SUCRA, equals (n - expected rank)/(n - 1), and equals the average over competitors of the probability of outranking each of them, so the pairwise table P(eta_i > eta_j), the rank intervals and SUCRA are one object seen three ways. Rücker and Schwarzer (2015) warn that the probability of being first ignores uncertainty and that these summaries add little over the intervals themselves; Trinquart et al. (2016) found that in 28% of published networks the top-ranked treatment had at least a 50% chance of not being the best. We therefore report rank intervals and P(rank <= k) in the appendix and carry the headline on A.

## 4. P(X > Y) as an effect size: common-language effect size, Vargha and Delaney's A, AUC, Mann-Whitney U, the exact test

Sources: [mcgraw1992cles], [vargha2000stochastic], [hanley1982roc], [bamber1975ordinal], [mann1947test], [wilcoxon1945ranking], [fay2010wmw], [bergmann2000packages], [ruscio2008probability], [scipy2026mannwhitneyu].

Quoted statements.

- McGraw and Wong 1992, abstract (OpenAlex, 2026-10-01): "a statistic that expresses how often a score sampled from one distribution will be greater than a score sampled from another distribution. The statistic, the common language effect size indicator, is easily calculated from sample means and variances (or from proportions in the case of nominal-level data)."
- Vargha and Delaney 2000, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "McGraw and Wong introduced this 'common language effect size statistic' for normal distributions and then proposed an approximate estimation for any continuous distribution. ... In the current paper a different generalization of CL, called the A measure of stochastic superiority, is proposed, which may be directly applied for any discrete or continuous variable that is at least ordinally scaled. Exact methods for point and interval estimation as well as the significance tests of the A = .5 hypothesis are provided."
- Hanley and McNeil 1982, abstract (PubMed PMID 7063747, 2026-10-01): "the area represents the probability that a randomly chosen diseased subject is (correctly) rated or ranked with greater suspicion than a randomly chosen non-diseased subject. Moreover, this probability of a correct ranking is the same quantity that is estimated by the already well-studied nonparametric Wilcoxon statistic." Bamber 1975 (title verified, Crossref, 2026-10-01): "The area above the ordinal dominance graph and the area below the receiver operating characteristic graph", the original proof of the identity.
- Mann and Whitney 1947, abstract (Project Euclid, 2026-10-01): "A statistic U depending on the relative ranks of the x's and y's is proposed for testing the hypothesis f = g." "tables have been computed giving the probability of U for samples up to n = m = 8"; "the limit distribution is normal if m, n go to infinity in any arbitrary manner".
- Fay and Proschan 2010, full text (PMC2857732, 2026-10-01), section 5.1.1: the Mann-Whitney functional, "defined (to make sense for discrete data as well)", phi(F, G) = Pr[Y_F > Y_G] + 1/2 Pr[Y_F = Y_G], which the WMW statistic estimates; "Wilcoxon (1945) proposed the exact WMW DR allowing for ties presenting the test as a permutation test on the sum of the ranks in one of the two groups."
- Bergmann, Ludbrook and Spooren 2000, abstract (OpenAlex, 2026-10-01): "the delivered p values ranged from significant to nonsignificant at the 5% level, depending on whether a large-sample approximation or an exact permutation form of the test was used and, in the former case, whether or not a correction for continuity was used and whether or not a correction for ties was made. ... We conclude that the only accurate form of the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney procedure is one in which the exact permutation null distribution is compiled for the actual data."
- Ruscio 2008, abstract (PubMed PMID 18331151, 2026-10-01): "The probability-based measure A, the nonparametric generalization of what K. O. McGraw and S. P. Wong (1992) called the common language effect size statistic, is insensitive to base rates and more robust to several other factors (e.g., extreme scores, nonlinear transformations)."
- scipy 1.18.0, scipy.stats.mannwhitneyu (docs.scipy.org, 2026-10-01): method 'exact' "computes the exact p-value by comparing the observed U statistic against the exact distribution of the U statistic under the null hypothesis"; method 'auto' "chooses 'exact' when the size of one of the samples is less than or equal to 8 and there are no ties; chooses 'asymptotic' otherwise."

Identities (derivation, standard). With m physical-AI and n frontier-model evaluations, U = sum_i sum_j [1(eta_i > eta_j) + 0.5 1(eta_i = eta_j)]. Then A = U/(mn) = P(eta_P > eta_F) + 0.5 P(eta_P = eta_F) = AUC of the ROC curve obtained by thresholding eta and treating group membership as the condition. A is invariant to any monotone transform of eta (log scale included), which is why it is the right scale-free comparison for values spanning decades and sitting at zero for half the set. Under H0: F = G, the distribution of U depends only on m and n, so the exact test is a permutation test; with ties (several evaluations at eta = 0) the exact null must be compiled for the actual data (Bergmann et al. 2000), which scipy's method='exact' does not do (it requires no ties), so the code should either implement the permutation over the actual values or state the asymptotic tie-corrected p alongside.

Why it matters for us. docs/QUESTIONS.md section 1 asserts that A is the AUC, equals U/(mn) and is the common-language effect size; the three quotes above are the citations, with Vargha and Delaney for the name A and the tie convention, and Ruscio for invariance to transformations. The exact-test remark is a practical constraint on the frequentist companion.

Paragraph for docs/QUESTIONS.md section 1. The probability that a randomly chosen physical-AI evaluation has higher eta than a randomly chosen frontier-model evaluation, ties counted one half, is Vargha and Delaney's (2000) A, the nonparametric form of McGraw and Wong's (1992) common-language effect size. It equals the Mann-Whitney U statistic divided by the number of pairs, and it equals the area under the ROC curve that treats "physical AI" as the condition and eta as the score (Bamber 1975; Hanley and McNeil 1982). Because A depends only on the order of the values it does not care that eta spans five decades or that it is exactly zero wherever the result cannot change the decision (Ruscio 2008). We report A at the central estimate and its distribution over the Monte Carlo draws; the exact Mann-Whitney test on the central estimates is the frequentist companion, computed by permutation over the actual values because of the ties at zero (Mann and Whitney 1947 tabulated the exact null only up to eight per group; Wilcoxon 1945 gave the permutation form that allows ties; Bergmann et al. 2000 show that approximations disagree at the 5% level on small samples).

Text not read.

Our model in these terms (derivation). The result can change the decision iff pi0 < pi* < pi1. Solving pi1(p) = pi* and pi0(p) = pi* gives the two thresholds of Pauker and Kassirer in regret form with zero test cost: the evaluation is worth running only when p_low < p < p_high, with p_low = pi*(1 - t) / (pi*(1 - t) + (1 - pi*) s) and p_high = pi* t / (pi* t + (1 - pi*)(1 - s)). Below p_low the developer deploys regardless, above p_high it responds regardless, and EVSI = 0 in both regimes (docs/DESIGN.md section 2). At the indifference prior p = pi*, EVPI = Lambda p(1 - p) and EVSI° = Lambda p(1 - p) J, so EVSI°/EVPI = J: Youden's index is the share of the perfect-information value that one run captures for an undecided developer, which is Hilden and Glasziou's "maximum proportional reduction in expected regret achieved by the test". Phelps and Mushlin's Hurdle 1 (assume a perfect test, i.e. EVPI, and screen out technologies that fail even then) is the same screen as our EVPI column.

Why it matters for us. docs/QUESTIONS.md section 6 explains the two zero regimes and the indifference value; Pauker and Kassirer 1980 is the citation that the structure is forty-five years old in medical decision making, and Hilden and Glasziou 1996 is the citation that J has exactly the meaning our EVSI° gives it. Howard 1966 and Raiffa and Schlaifer 1961 are the origin of VOI.

Paragraph for docs/QUESTIONS.md section 6. The model is the classic one for a diagnostic test (Raiffa and Schlaifer 1961; Howard 1966). Pauker and Kassirer (1975, 1980) showed that a binary test is worth performing only when the pre-test probability lies between two thresholds: below the testing threshold the clinician withholds treatment whatever the result, above the test-treatment threshold the clinician treats without testing, and in both cases the test has no value because its result cannot cross the treatment threshold. Our "deploys regardless" and "responds regardless" are those two regimes, and the condition pi0 < pi* < pi1 is their interval written in posteriors. Youden's (1950) index J = s + t - 1 enters through the indifference value: for a developer who is exactly undecided, the value of perfect information is (B + K) p(1 - p) and one run of the evaluation captures the fraction J of it, which Hilden and Glasziou (1996) derived as the clinical meaning of J, "the maximum proportional reduction in expected regret achieved by the test".

Mapping (derivation). Health economics prices research as per-patient EVSI times the number of patients the decision covers over its horizon, minus the cost of the research; Claxton and Posnett (1996) and Willan and Pinto (2005) choose the design that maximises that difference, Phelps and Mushlin (1988) "add across the eligible population", and Philips, Claxton and Palmer (2008) show the horizon is the weak input. Our net_n = n EVSI - C_build - n C_run is the same object with deployment decisions in place of patients and with a per-run cost; n* = C_build/(EVSI - C_run) is the break-even count, infinite when one run costs more than it is worth; P(pays) = P(n >= n*). Eliciting n instead of assuming a horizon is our answer to Philips et al.; their finding that the value of the research declines as technologies change is the reason n is bounded (an evaluation of a model family goes stale as the family changes). Eckermann and Willan's cost of reversal corresponds to the asymmetry between delaying a release and recalling a deployed system, which the developer perspective of B and K absorbs. The SHM literature prices a monitoring system by the decisions it informs over the service life (Thöns 2018) and ranks sensing systems by VOI against their cost (Pozzi and Der Kiureghian 2011), the same comparison as our eta.

Why it matters for us. docs/QUESTIONS.md section 4 introduces C_build, C_run, n, n* and P(pays) without precedent; these are the precedents. The paper can say in one sentence that the construction is the population EVSI and expected net benefit of sampling of health technology assessment, applied to evaluations instead of trials.

Paragraph for docs/QUESTIONS.md section 4. An evaluation, like a clinical trial or a bridge monitoring system, is paid for once and informs many decisions. Health technology assessment prices a trial as the per-patient value of its information times the population the decision covers over its horizon, net of the cost of the trial (Claxton and Posnett 1996; Willan and Pinto 2005; Phelps and Mushlin 1988), and the choice of horizon is the input that moves the answer most (Philips, Claxton and Palmer 2008). Structural health monitoring prices a sensing system by the maintenance decisions it will inform over the service life against its cost (Pozzi and Der Kiureghian 2011; Thöns 2018). Our version replaces the horizon with an elicited reuse count n, the number of distinct deployment decisions the built evaluation will inform: the lifetime value is n EVSI - C_build - n C_run, building pays after n* = C_build/(EVSI - C_run) decisions, and P(pays) is the probability that the elicited n exceeds n*. The reuse count is also where obsolescence enters: an evaluation of a model family loses value as the family changes, which caps n.

## 7. Log-scale elicitation and the lognormal fit to three quantiles

Quoted statements.

- Keefer and Bodily 1983, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "Numerical results from estimating means and variances of a set of beta distributions indicate surprisingly large differences in accuracy among approximations in current use, with some of the most popular ones such as the PERT and triangular-density-function approximations faring poorly. A simple new three-point approximation, which is a straightforward extension of earlier work by Pearson and Tukey, outperforms the others significantly in these tests". The weights, quoted from Wang, Dyer and Butler 2016 (Risk Analysis 36(2):396-410, open preprint text, 2026-10-01): "the EPT method approximates a continuous distribution using a three-point discrete distribution with probabilities 0.185, 0.630, and 0.185 assigned to the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles of the continuous distribution." Keefer 1994, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "three-point discrete-distribution approximations exist that match the first several moments of an underlying distribution exactly while, counterintuitively, approximating its certainty equivalents poorly."
- Spetzler and Staël von Holstein 1975, abstract (Crossref, 2026-10-01): "Probability encoding, the process of extracting and quantifying individual judgment about uncertain quantities, is one of the major functions required in the performance of decision analysis. ... The authors recommend and describe a structured interview process that utilizes a trained interviewer and a number of techniques designed to reduce biases and aid in the quantification of judgment."
- Colson and Cooke 2018, full text (2026-10-01): "experts typically state their fifth, fiftieth, and ninety-fifth percentiles for the estimate of each uncertain item (Cooke 1991; Cooke and Goossens 2008). The fiftieth percentile is the median estimate ... The fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles create a ninety percent credible range". Footnote 9: "The background distribution is chosen by the analyst, with the standard choice being a uniform (or log-uniform) distribution that includes the range of values provided by the experts". The log-uniform background is how the classical model treats quantities that span orders of magnitude: it scores experts on the log scale.
- Limpert, Stahel and Abbt 2001, full text pp. 344-345 (author copy, ETH, 2026-10-01): "A random variable X is log-normally distributed if log(X) has a normal distribution." "For normally distributed data, the interval mu +/- sigma covers a probability of 68.3%, while mu +/- 2 sigma covers 95.5%. The corresponding statements for log-normal quantities are [mu*/sigma*, mu* sigma*] = mu* x/ sigma* (contains 68.3%) and [mu*/(sigma*)^2, mu* (sigma*)^2] = mu* x/ (sigma*)^2 (contains 95.5%). This characterization shows that the operations of multiplying and dividing, which we denote with the sign x/ (times/divide), help to determine useful intervals for log-normal distributions (Figure 3), in the same way that the operations of adding and subtracting (+/-, or plus/minus) do for normal distributions." "For quantities with a log-normal distribution, however, multiplication is the relevant operation for combining them in most applications ... The product of independent log-normal quantities also follows a log-normal distribution. The median of this product is the product of the medians of its factors." "For a log-normal distribution, the most precise (i.e., asymptotically most efficient) method for estimating the parameters mu* and sigma* relies on log transformation."
- SHELF 1.13.0 manual (2026-10-01): fitdist "fits parametric distributions using least squares on the cumulative distribution function", with the log-normal among the families. MATCH tool page (Nottingham, 2026-10-01) lists the fitted families "Normal, Student-t, Scaled Beta, Gamma, Log Normal, and Log Student-t"; Morris, Oakley and Crowe 2014, abstract (OpenAlex, 2026-10-01): "The Tool is free to use, and offers five different techniques for eliciting univariate probability distributions."
- O'Hagan 2019, abstract (OpenAlex, 2026-10-01): "One such practice is to follow a recognized protocol that is designed to address and minimize the cognitive biases that experts are prone to when making probabilistic judgments." Garthwaite, Kadane and O'Hagan 2005, abstract (Semantic Scholar, 2026-10-01): "The psychological literature suggests that people are prone to certain heuristics and biases in how they respond to situations involving uncertainty. As a result, some of the ways of asking questions about uncertain quantities are preferable to others, and appear to be more reliable."

The fit, as the code does it (voi_rank/fit.py, read 2026-10-01; derivation). For ln X ~ N(mu, sigma^2) the three elicited quantiles are q05 = exp(mu - 1.6449 sigma), q50 = exp(mu), q95 = exp(mu + 1.6449 sigma), so a lognormal passes through all three only when q95/q50 = q50/q05, i.e. when the triple is symmetric on the log scale. Least squares on the log quantiles is linear in the data and gives mu = mean(ln q05, ln q50, ln q95) and sigma = (ln q95 - ln q05)/(2 x 1.6449) exactly; the residual is the log-asymmetry, and fit.py flags it when it exceeds 0.25 of the log range. An elicitor who answers (1, 10, 1000) is saying the upper half is ten times wider than the lower half on the log scale; the lognormal cannot honour that; the stored residual records it. In Limpert's notation sigma* = exp(sigma) and the elicited 90% range is mu* x/ (sigma*)^1.645. The mean exp(mu + sigma^2/2) sits above the median by exp(sigma^2/2), a factor 2.7 when q95/q05 = 100 and 9.1 when it is 1000, which is why the central estimate at medians and the EVSI at pooled means (item 2) differ for B, K and the costs. The Beta fit for p, s, t is least squares on the raw quantiles with a moment-matched start (sd = (q95 - q05)/3.29), two parameters to three constraints, residual flagged above 0.02.

Three-point approximations as a check (derivation). The extended Pearson-Tukey rule E[X] ~ 0.185 q05 + 0.630 q50 + 0.185 q95 (Keefer and Bodily 1983) is the decision-analysis shortcut for the mean from exactly the three numbers we elicit, and (q95 - q05)/3.29 is the normal-theory standard deviation. We do not need the shortcut because the Monte Carlo draws the fitted distribution, but the EPT mean is a cheap sanity check on each fit: a lognormal fit whose mean is far from the EPT mean of its own triple is one whose triple is strongly asymmetric. Keefer and Bodily's finding that the triangular and PERT approximations fare poorly is the reason not to fit a triangular distribution to the three points.

Why it matters for us. docs/DESIGN.md section 4 fixes the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles and the lognormal fit for USD quantities and n; the 5/50/95 convention is the classical model's (Cooke 1991; Colson and Cooke 2018), the lognormal is the right family for positive quantities spanning orders of magnitude and combining multiplicatively (Limpert et al. 2001), and least squares on the quantiles is what SHELF and MATCH do. The log-asymmetry flag is the honest residual of an over-determined fit. The pilots' finding that a log-decade framing did not reduce spread (docs/QUESTIONS.md section 4) is consistent with the elicitation literature's view that the noise comes from the elicitor, not the question's shape (Clemen and Winkler 1999; Hora 2004).

Paragraph for docs/QUESTIONS.md (fitting, section 5, and number formats). We ask for the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles because that is the convention of structured expert judgement (Cooke 1991; Colson and Cooke 2018): the median splits the elicitor's belief in half and the outer two bound a 90% credible range, which is also the triple the classical three-point approximations of decision analysis use (Pearson and Tukey 1965; Keefer and Bodily 1983). For dollar amounts and the reuse count we fit a lognormal, the distribution of a positive quantity whose uncertainty is multiplicative, described by a median and a factor rather than a mean and a difference (Limpert, Stahel and Abbt 2001). Three quantiles over-determine its two parameters; least squares on the log quantiles gives the median's log as the mean of the three logs and the log-width as the 5-to-95 log range divided by 3.29, and leaves a residual equal to the asymmetry of the triple on the log scale, which we store and flag above a quarter of the range. This is the same least-squares fit the SHELF and MATCH elicitation tools perform (Oakley 2026; Morris, Oakley and Crowe 2014). For probabilities we fit a Beta the same way. Because the lognormal mean exceeds its median by exp(sigma^2/2), a factor 2.7 for a hundredfold 90% range, point summaries at medians and expectations over draws answer different questions, and the paper says which it reports.
