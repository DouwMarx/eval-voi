\section{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

\emph{Developer perspective.} The headline values $B$ and $K$ for society. A developer may weigh only its own exposure: liability, recall, regulatory action, reputation and lost revenue. We elicit the decision prompt again under that perspective and keep the instrument parameters of the headline run.
\ifdev

\emph{Run-only efficiency.} A developer who adopts a published evaluation pays only the run. Its efficiency is $\eta_\mathrm{run}=\EVSI/C_\mathrm{run}$, the value per dollar to an adopter of an evaluation that already exists. Under $\eta_\mathrm{run}$ a random physical-AI evaluation beats a random LLM evaluation with probability \voiEtaRunACentral{} at the central estimate (90\% interval over draws \voiEtaRunAQLo{} to \voiEtaRunAQHi{}; exact Mann-Whitney $p=\voiEtaRunMWp$), against \voiEtaACentral{} under $\eta$. % CHECK-DATA
Removing the build cost does not lift the physical-AI evaluations, consistent with build costs that are similar in the two groups. % CHECK-DATA

\emph{Indifference value.} Under $\eta^\circ=\EVSI^\circ/C$ the probability is \voiEtaIndACentral{} (90\% interval \voiEtaIndAQLo{} to \voiEtaIndAQHi{}; $p=\voiEtaIndMWp$).

\emph{Sensitivity.} Averaged over evaluations, the absolute Spearman correlation of each parameter's draws with $\eta$ is \voiRhoP{} for $p$, \voiRhoS{} for $s$, \voiRhoT{} for $t$, \voiRhoB{} for $B$, \voiRhoK{} for $K$, \voiRhoCBuild{} for $C_\mathrm{build}$, \voiRhoCRun{} for $C_\mathrm{run}$ and \voiRhoN{} for $n$ ($n$ does not enter $\eta$; its row checks that the draws are independent).

\emph{Planned ablations, not yet run.} Numeric anchors in the prompts (the template has an optional block, empty in the headline); the context mode (curated facts only, facts plus source abstracts, facts plus full source text); drawing whole elicitations instead of independent parameters, which keeps each member's story coherent; and the number of evaluations per group.

\section{Elicitation details}
\label{sec:elicitation}

\emph{Prompts.} Each evaluation gets two prompts, rendered from the two templates below. The decision prompt fills in the agent, the decision, the definition of $\theta$ and the decision facts; it never describes the evaluation. The instrument prompt fills in the evaluation's title, agent, decision, definition of $\theta$, the instrument and the instrument facts; it never states $p$, $B$ or $K$. The two are separate calls because a single prompt lets the instrument text move the elicited prior. The perspective (society or developer) is a template variable set by the protocol. The facts are sentences taken from the cited sources, each traceable to a cached copy of its source text.

\emph{Order and format.} Per parameter, the member writes a short reasoning paragraph, then the 5th and 95th percentiles as exclusion questions ("a value you would be surprised to see the true value fall below or above"), then the median. Extremes first is standard practice against anchoring on the best guess~\citep{ohagan2006uncertain}. Numbers are plain decimals; the parser also reads scientific notation.

\emph{Validation.} An answer is valid when it is strict JSON with exactly the parameters of its prompt, each with $p_5<p_{50}<p_{95}$; probabilities lie in $(0,1)$; USD amounts and $n$ are positive and $n\ge1$ at the median; the median $s$ exceeds one minus the median $t$ (the evaluation is informative); and the prior's median lies in $[0.001,0.999]$. A failed attempt is retried once. Failures are classed as unparseable JSON, wrong schema, a violated constraint, a failed fit, a refusal (no JSON and the text declines, or a content filter), a truncated answer, or a transport error.

\emph{Fitting and pooling.} Each elicitation's three percentiles are fitted by least squares on the quantiles: a Beta distribution for $p$, $s$ and $t$, a lognormal for $B$, $K$, $C_\mathrm{build}$, $C_\mathrm{run}$ and $n$. Two parameters fitted to three quantiles leave a residual, stored as a quality flag. The pooled belief is the equal-weight mixture of the fitted distributions over every valid elicitation, the linear opinion pool~\citep{stone1961opinionpool,clemen1999combining}. A single distribution fitted to all members' quantiles would be forced to one mode and one width; the mixture keeps disagreement visible, which is what the uncertainty statements should carry.

\emph{Members and health.} Table~\ref{tab:health} gives attempts, the valid share, the failures by class and the cost per member. Of \voiAttempts{} attempts, \voiValidShare{} were valid, \voiInvalidRefusal{} were refused, \voiInvalidJson{} were unparseable and \voiInvalidConstraint{} violated a constraint; the run cost \voiUSD.

\section{The final ensemble (planned run)}
\label{sec:final}

The final run uses six members from six developers through one routing service, one call per member and prompt, the society perspective, no numeric anchors and the curated facts. Three members come from Chinese developers and three from US developers. Members were chosen for developer diversity, reliable JSON output and price. Some evaluations come from member developers (Google DeepMind's ASIMOV and Veo evaluations), and three LLM evaluations from Anthropic, which has no member; we treat this as a limitation, not a correction. Each member is pinned to one endpoint with fallbacks disabled, so the weights and the price cannot change during the run; endpoints were chosen among those that keep no data. Reasoning effort is set low for the two members that reason longest at their default and medium for the others. Requests ask for a JSON object and refuse endpoints that ignore the request options.

Why many members and no repeats. In the pilots the variance between members was comparable to the variance between repeats of one member, even with members from one developer, so a call spent on a new member buys at least as much as a call spent on a repeat. A diverse crowd of LLMs forecasts about as well as a human crowd~\citep{schoenegger2024wisdom}, and models of different developers make less correlated errors than models of one developer~\citep{kim2025correlated}. The counter-argument, that a few strong models beat a mix, assumes a verifier that tells which model is best; for elicited priors there is none.

Cost. The dry run of the protocol estimates USD 5.7 for the headline run (low USD 3.1, high USD 11) from the routing service's published prices and token counts measured in a smoke test, and USD 2.8 (low USD 1.6, high USD 5.4) for the developer-perspective decision prompt. % SOURCE: voi_rank.elicit --dry-run --protocol final (and final_dev --stage decision), catalogue of 2026-10-01; not a generated macro
Prompt caching saves little at one call per member and prompt, and the estimate ignores it.

\section{The most damaging things a reviewer could say}
\label{sec:reviewer}

Each objection is stated as a reviewer would put it, followed by our answer.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{``Language models made up the numbers.''} They did, and the paper says so. The claim is comparative: where one group sits relative to the other, under one set of beliefs applied to both. Every member answers every evaluation from the same template and the same kind of sourced facts, so a bias common to all evaluations moves both groups. A bias specific to physical-AI knowledge would not cancel, and the Monte Carlo interval does not capture it, because members are drawn independently per evaluation; a leave-one-member-out analysis is the planned check. Where a measured sensitivity, specificity or cost exists, it can replace the elicited one without changing the method.
\item \emph{``The members are one population. Their agreement proves nothing.''} Agreement between models trained on similar data cannot separate shared knowledge from shared bias~\citep{kim2025correlated}. The analysed run uses two Claude models, so this objection applies in full; the planned final ensemble uses six models from six other developers (Section~\ref{sec:final}). We report cross-member agreement only as evidence about the inputs (Fig.~\ref{fig:members}). It remains a limitation.
\item \emph{``You wrote the decisions, so you rank the decisions, not the evaluations.''} The pilots found exactly this: most of the variance of the indifference value per dollar came from the stakes per dollar of the decision attached to each evaluation. Here each decision is the release decision the evaluation's own sources describe, written to a fixed schema, and the decision prompt never sees the evaluation. The question asked is the one a developer faces: what is this evaluation worth for the decision it is used for. Pricing physical-AI and LLM evaluations against one shared decision, matched pairs, would separate the two.
\item \emph{``A binary pass or fail is a caricature of an evaluation that returns a score.''} The model is deliberately simple and aims at orders of magnitude and rankings. A score read against a threshold set for the decision can only widen the range of priors for which the result changes the decision. The indifference value, which does not depend on the threshold, places the groups the same way and more sharply (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}). % CHECK-DATA
\item \emph{``Half of your physical-AI evaluations test a language model inside a robot, and one is a car.''} That is what published physical-AI safety evaluation looks like today. The set spans fidelity levels from text question answering to track testing, and the fidelity analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:level}) uses that spread.
\item \emph{``The groups are small and chosen by different rules.''} The two lists are ranked by fixed inclusion rules, and the test is exact. It separates the groups despite low power and many ties, and the separation has a named cause, the stakes per decision, that a reader can check against Figure~\ref{fig:params}. % CHECK-DATA
\item \emph{``Society's stakes are arbitrary.''} The developer-perspective ablation re-elicits $B$ and $K$ as the developer's own exposure (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}).
\item \emph{``Many evaluations have zero value, so the ranking is mostly ties.''} Ties count as one half in every comparison, and the zeros are themselves a finding: for those decisions the developer should mitigate first and evaluate the mitigated system. The indifference value has no zeros and gives a full ranking.
\item \emph{``Drawing parameters independently distorts the uncertainty.''} It does, in a direction we cannot sign: EVSI is not monotone in $p$, $B$ and $K$, and independence across evaluations narrows the interval of $A$. The planned ablation that draws whole elicitations measures the effect.
\item \emph{``Models refuse biosecurity and cyber prompts, so the sample is biased.''} Refusals are counted per member and class (Table~\ref{tab:health}); a slot with no valid answer after one retry is missing, not imputed.
\item \emph{``The context is cherry-picked.''} The facts are sentences we selected from the sources, so selection is possible. Every fact cites a source whose text is fetched programmatically and cached in the repository, the same facts go to every member, and the planned context-mode ablation replaces our selection with the sources' abstracts or full text.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What the pilots found}
\label{sec:pilots}

Two pilot studies preceded this one, with fewer evaluations and members from a single developer. The first priced ten LLM and five physical-AI evaluations. Its ranking by indifference value per dollar was stable across members, but mostly because the members agreed on the stakes of the decisions; whether a single result could change the decision varied between members. With five physical-AI evaluations it could not place the groups. The second priced evaluations of increasing fidelity for two fixed decisions. A single prompt let the instrument description move the elicited prior; separate decision and instrument prompts removed that effect. Its indifference value per dollar fell with fidelity level on both decisions. This study adopts both lessons: separate prompts, more evaluations per group, members from several developers, and fidelity as one discussion point.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

Two extensions follow directly from the model. \emph{Growth over time}: as robot fleets grow, the stakes of a physical-AI release and the number of decisions a built evaluation informs grow with them, and the scale paragraph of Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the factor at which the comparison flips. \emph{Other decision-makers}: a regulator permitting deployment, a funder building an evaluation for the field (whose value is $n\,\EVSI-C_\mathrm{build}-n\,C_\mathrm{run}$), and an insurer setting a premium. The project's future-work notes (docs/FUTURE\_WORK.md in the repository) list these in more detail.
