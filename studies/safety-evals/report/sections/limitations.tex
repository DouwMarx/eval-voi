\section{Limitations and conclusion}
\label{sec:limitations}

Every parameter is an LLM's belief. Despite the precautions (\voiNMembers{} models from \voiNMembers{} developers, none of them the developer of the fact-curating model; reasoned distributions rather than points; the same sourced facts for every member), the parameters are not validated against human experts, LLM intervals are often too narrow~\citep{hobor2026bayesian}, and no evaluation publishes a sensitivity and specificity against real harm. The curated facts are a second weak point: an agent chose which sentences of the sources to show, and the elicited numbers may lean on them.\ifdefined\voinoctxEtaStarPSQMed{} With no facts shown at all, $\PS$ by $\etast$ moves from \voiEtaStarPSQMed{} to \voinoctxEtaStarPSQMed{} and the stakes ratio from \voiStakesRatio{} to \voinoctxStakesRatio\ifextended{} (Section~\ref{sec:noctx})\fi.\fi{} % CHECK-DATA
Each decision is written from the evaluation's own sources, so the comparison is between the decisions these evaluations inform as much as between the evaluations; pricing both groups against one shared decision would separate the two, and the groups are not matched pairs by construction. Published physical-AI safety evaluations are few and uneven (most of the \voiNPhysical{} test a language or vision-language model inside a robot, one is a vehicle), so the result also describes today's evaluations. Finally, the model reduces an evaluation to one binary result for one release.

\label{sec:conclusion}
In sum, physical-AI safety evaluations are worth less per dollar than LLM safety evaluations because far less is at stake per release today, not because they cost more or measure worse; even the best of them rarely reaches the top ten, and the gap closes only as the stakes of physical-AI releases grow with deployment.
