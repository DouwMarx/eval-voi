\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Developers of large language models (LLMs) decide releases on safety evaluations~\citep{shevlane2023extreme}, under published frameworks~\citep{anthropic2026rsp,openai2025preparedness}. A virology question set such as the Virology Capabilities Test (VCT)~\citep{gotting2025vct} runs in minutes against an application programming interface. Physical-AI safety evaluations test a robot, a vehicle or a model that controls one. They range from question answering about physical danger~\citep{jindal2025danger} and simulated household tasks~\citep{yin2024safeagentbench} to world-model rollouts~\citep{geminirobotics2025veo}, jailbreaks of real robots~\citep{robey2024robopair} and vehicle track protocols~\citep{iihs2024paeb}. Many need hardware, scenes, test tracks and staff. The common intuition is that they cost too much to pay off in the way a biosecurity question set does.

Cost alone cannot settle this. An evaluation is worth what it changes: a cheap test whose result cannot move the release decision is worth nothing. Decision analysis prices this with the expected value of sample information (EVSI)~\citep{raiffa1961applied,howard1966information}, which health economics uses to decide which studies to fund~\citep{claxton1999irrelevance}. The cost of AI evaluations is now tracked~\citep{ghosh2026evalcosts}, and robot learning asks whether cheap simulated evaluations predict real ones~\citep{li2024simpler}. We know of no work that prices a safety evaluation by the decision it changes.

We price \voiNPhysical{} physical-AI and \voiNLLM{} LLM safety evaluations on one scale, the decision value of one run per dollar spent, and ask where the physical-AI ones sit. We compare groups, since elicited inputs are more reliable in relative order than in size. Figure~\ref{fig:headline} gives the answer: lower, because the decisions they inform carry less at stake, not because they cost more. % CHECK-DATA
