\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Developers of large language models (LLMs) run safety evaluations before a release and report them in system cards~\citep{shevlane2023extreme,anthropic2026rsp,openai2025preparedness}. A virology question set~\citep{vct-paper} runs in minutes through a programming interface. Physical-AI safety evaluations test a robot, a vehicle or a model that controls one, from world-model rollouts~\citep{geminirobotics2025veo} to jailbreaks of real robots~\citep{robey2024robopair} and vehicle track protocols~\citep{iihs_paeb}. They need hardware, scenes and staff, so they look expensive relative to the information they return.

Cost alone does not determine whether an evaluation is worth running. An evaluation is a measurement instrument, and the observations it produces are worth money only when they can change a decision. Decision analysis quantifies this as the expected value of sample information (EVSI)~\citep{raiffa1961applied,howard1966information}, used in health economics to choose which studies to fund~\citep{claxton1999irrelevance}. To our knowledge it has not been applied to AI safety evaluations, where a finite budget also has to be spent where it reduces harm most.

We estimate the value of information per unit cost of \voiNPhysical{} physical-AI safety evaluations and compare it with that of \voiNLLM{} LLM safety evaluations commonly cited in system cards. The parameters of the decision model are elicited as probability distributions from an ensemble of \voiNMembers{} LLMs, and Monte Carlo draws carry their uncertainty into rankings and group comparisons. We report rankings and comparisons rather than absolute values, to account for the limitations of LLM-based parameter elicitation.
